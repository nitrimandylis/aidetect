The Dreyfus Affair unfolded within a deeply fractured Third Republic, where fragile democratic institutions confronted resurgent anti-Semitism, revanchist nationalism, and entrenched military authoritarianism. The humiliation of the 1871 Franco-Prussian War fueled a revanchist public eager to restore French honor, while republicans and monarchists remained locked in political struggle following the 1889 centennial. The army, seen as guardian of national pride, enjoyed near-untouchable prestige, and its officer corps was predominantly Catholic and conservative. Simultaneously, a small but influential Jewish bourgeoisie became a convenient scapegoat for economic anxieties. This volatile climate allowed the wrongful conviction of Alfred Dreyfus to become a polarized symbol of republican justice versus militarist tradition, exposing deep social divisions.
