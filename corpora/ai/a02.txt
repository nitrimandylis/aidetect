Christopher Marlowe’s *Doctor Faustus* both inherits and subverts the medieval morality‑play framework, revealing the complex shift in early‑modern attitudes toward spirituality. While the play retains the conventional dichotomy of a sinful protagonist and allegorical forces—Mephistopheles as a devilish tempter and the Good and Evil Angels as conscience—its protagonist’s intellectual hubris and desire for boundless knowledge betray Renaissance humanism’s emphasis on individual agency. Unlike the didactic certainty of medieval exempla, Faustus’s salvation remains ambiguous, his final torment oscillating between divine mercy and inexorable damnation, reflecting contemporary anxieties about the erosion of ecclesiastical authority and the emergence of secular epistemologies. Thus, Marlowe dramatizes a transitional spirituality wherein moral instruction is entwined with existential doubt.
