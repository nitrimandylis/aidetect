While both Marlowe and Goethe dramatize a longing that transcends worldly knowledge, their texts ultimately diverge in their appraisal of human spirituality. Marlowe’s Faustus, bounded by a rigidly Christian cosmos, finds his spiritual yearning perverted into a fatal transaction; his desire for “a sound magician” and “a deity” is ultimately a quest for power, and his spiritual dimension manifests only as the terrifying certainty of damnation that torments his final hour. Conversely, Goethe’s Faust, operating within a more expansive, humanistic framework, embarks on a quest for a form of spiritual fulfillment that integrates rather than denies earthly experience. His striving, though it leads through error and guilt, is presented as a dynamic principle of spiritual growth, not inherent sin.
