Ultimately, Émile Zola's "J'accuse" proved decisive in transforming the Dreyfus Affair from a disputed military verdict into a national struggle over justice, truth, and the Republic itself. By sacrificing his own safety and reputation, Zola demonstrated that intellectuals could intervene directly in political life, galvanizing public opinion and forcing the reopening of Dreyfus's case. Although the letter did not immediately secure Dreyfus's exoneration, it exposed the conspiracies and antisemitism underlying his conviction, while deepening the conflict between Dreyfusards and anti-Dreyfusards. The affair reshaped French society, contributing to the separation of church and state and establishing the writer's moral duty to bear witness. Zola's manifesto thus stands as a landmark in the history of press freedom, human rights, and engaged literature.
