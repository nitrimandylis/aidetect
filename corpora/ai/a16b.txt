At the core of this analysis lies a crucial distinction between deconstruction as a rigorous philosophical practice and its popular misappropriation as a tool for cultural relativism. Derrida’s critique of logocentrism and the metaphysics of presence was never a cynical denial of meaning but a meticulous excavation of the unstable ground upon which meaning is constructed. The practical postmodernism adopted by some proponents of multiculturalism, however, commits a fundamental fallacy by conflating the deconstruction of a dominant narrative with the automatic validation of all marginalized ones. This move effectively reifies difference, transforming deconstruction’s endless deferral of meaning into a rigid, unassailable hierarchy of identity claims. Consequently, the radical ethical impulse of Derrida’s work—a responsibility to the absolute singularity of the other—is paradoxically neutralized, becoming a logic of segregated cultural enclaves rather than a call for a more just and open negotiation of difference.
