In both Johann Wolfgang von Goethe’s Faust and Christopher Marlowe’s Doctor Faustus, the protagonists’ engagements with spiritual matters reveal starkly divergent cultural attitudes toward the human soul’s quest for transcendence; while Goethe’s Faust, steeped in a synthesis of Romantic idealism and Enlightenment rationalism, seeks redemption through an ever‑expanding experiential communion with the divine, Marlowe’s Faustus embraces a Faustian bargain that reifies a crisis of faith and a nihilistic repudiation of Christian morality, thereby exposing the play’s preoccupation with the fragility of spiritual certainty. This essay contends that these contrasting portrayals reflect their respective historical contexts — Enlightenment optimism versus Counter‑Reformation anxieties — and illuminate how each author negotiates the tension between human aspiration and divine limitation."
