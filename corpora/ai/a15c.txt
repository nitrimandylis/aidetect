In sum, Kant’s treatment of the suicide maxim reveals the rigorous demands of perfect duty: the categorical imperative obliges us to preserve life not merely as a contingent means, but as an end in itself, grounded in the universal law of self‑legislation. While the maxim’s self‑contradiction underscores the incoherence of consenting to one’s own annihilation, the broader moral architecture of the Groundwork affirms that duty transcends consequentialist calculations and demands respect for rational agents’ intrinsic worth. Consequently, suicide emerges not merely as a personal choice but as a violation of the very condition that makes moral agency possible, thereby reaffirming the centrality of perfect duty in Kantian ethics.
