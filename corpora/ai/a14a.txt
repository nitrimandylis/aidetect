The introduction establishes the premise that Friedrich Nietzsche’s critique of religion often presupposes a rigid dualism between life-affirming and life-denying impulses, a framework he applies broadly to ascetic traditions. This essay challenges the universality of that binary by examining Japanese Buddhism as a significant counter-example. While Nietzsche viewed Buddhism primarily through the lens of early Indian texts, which he interpreted as a passive yearning for non-existence, the historical development of schools such as Zen, Pure Land, and Shingon reveals a dynamic engagement with the material world, aesthetics, and even martial culture. By analyzing the integration of Buddhist principles into daily life and artistic expression in Japan, this investigation questions whether the Nietzschean dichotomy is sufficient to explain a tradition that simultaneously embraces impermanence and profound worldly vitality.
