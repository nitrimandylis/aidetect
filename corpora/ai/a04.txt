The institutionalisation of football in early twentieth-century Costa Rica functioned as a strategic apparatus for consolidating liberal state hegemony following the disruptive caudillo era. By sponsoring municipal clubs and constructing centralised playing fields, governing elites redirected potentially volatile urban male energies into codified competition under official supervision, thereby inculcating discipline, punctuality, and respect for hierarchical authority. Simultaneously, the spectacle of local teams, such as Liga Deportiva Alajuelense or CS Herediano, fostered a nascent national consciousness that transcended regional loyalties. When these clubs contested championships, spectators could enact a shared, bounded civic identity—distinct from Nicaragua or Panama—without directly challenging the socioeconomic structures that persisted. Thus, football operated as a subtle instrument of social control, manufacturing consent through patriotic and recreational participation.
