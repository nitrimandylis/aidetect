Austen’s manipulation of outdoor spaces in *Emma* consistently externalizes the internal liberties and constraints of her characters, most notably through the contrast between the open, unregulated walks of Highbury’s countryside and the manicured, socially policed grounds of Donwell Abbey and Hartfield. While Emma, whose wealth and status afford her physical mobility, readily traverses these spaces, her perceptual errors—misreading Harriet’s attachment to the farmer Robert Martin during a ramble or misconstruing Frank Churchill’s gallantry at Box Hill—occur precisely when she feels most empowered by the freedom of the outdoors. Conversely, Jane Fairfax’s exhausting trek to the post office reveals her profound lack of agency, as the very landscape that promises invigorating liberty becomes a stage for her confinement, demonstrating that in Austen’s world, one’s relationship to nature is not a simple escape from society but a direct reflection of one’s position within its hierarchy.
