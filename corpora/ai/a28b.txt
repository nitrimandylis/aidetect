The appropriation of the African Mbangu mask in Picasso’s *Les Demoiselles d’Avignon* reveals a fraught convergence of artistic ambition and colonial contextualization. By foregrounding the mask’s stylized geometry, Picasso translates a sacred object into a conduit for avant‑garde fragmentation, yet the painting simultaneously commodifies a lived cultural practice. Contemporary scholarship notes that Picasso accessed the mask through ethnographic exhibits, bypassing its ritual significance and reducing it to a formal device. This act of borrowing, however, is not uniformly exploitative; it also destabilizes Western representational norms and provokes a dialogue on creative cross‑pollination. Consequently, the work embodies both a bold cultural transposition and a contested ethical terrain for modern artistic discourse.
