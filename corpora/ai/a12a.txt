The fraught relationship between Japanese imperial policy and Jewish populations seeking refuge in Asia during the Second World War represents a complex intersection of geopolitical strategy, racial ideology, and pragmatic opportunism. This essay investigates the evolution of Japanese policy towards Jewish refugees in Shanghai between 1938 and 1943, a period marked by initial tolerance, formalized ghettoization, and shifting strategic calculations. While often framed as a monolithic or altruistic endeavor—encapsulated in the so-called “Fugu Plan”—the reality was far more contingent and fractured. This investigation will explore the extent to which Japanese policy demonstrated continuity in its underlying pragmatism and anti-Semitic underpinnings, and change in its operational implementation, arguing that the shift from relative laissez-faire acceptance to strict ghettoization was driven by the changing tide of the war and pressure from Nazi Germany, rather than a fundamental ideological shift.
