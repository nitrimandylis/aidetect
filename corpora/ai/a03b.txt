Throughout An Imaginary Life, the dialogue between language and nature functions as the novel's central mechanism for dramatising Ovid's transformation from Roman rhetorician to attuned inhabitant of place. Initially, language is possessive and imperial: Ovid "names" objects to impose order upon an alien landscape, echoing Rome's colonising gaze. Yet as the Child unteaches him, Malouf erodes this mastery—words loosen, silence becomes communicative, and nature itself acquires voice. The past tense narration, collapsing into the immediate present at the novel's close, enacts this shift formally, as if language dissolves into the very landscape it once described. Thus the dialogue operates not merely thematically but structurally: Malouf suggests that genuine selfhood emerges only when language ceases dominating nature and instead listens, enabling the transcendence through "imaginative sympathy" that the novel ultimately celebrates.
