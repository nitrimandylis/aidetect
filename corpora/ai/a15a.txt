In the *Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals*, Immanuel Kant establishes the categorical imperative as the supreme principle of morality, grounding duty in the rational consistency of a universalizable maxim. A central and provocative application of this principle is his condemnation of suicide, which he argues stems from a maxim of self-love that contradicts itself when willed as a universal law of nature. This essay will critically examine Kant’s argument that the prohibition of suicide constitutes a perfect duty to oneself, assessing its logical coherence within his broader ethical framework. By interrogating the nature of the contradiction he identifies and the distinction between perfect and imperfect duties, this investigation seeks to clarify the rigorous demands and potential limitations of Kantian moral philosophy regarding the autonomous individual’s dominion over their own life.
