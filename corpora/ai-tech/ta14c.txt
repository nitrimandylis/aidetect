In sum, the investigation reveals that the theoretical promise of maximal braking on a freeride bicycle is continually curtailed by an intertwined set of mechanical, environmental and rider‑related limitations: tire‑to‑ground friction cannot consistently achieve its upper bound under varied surface conditions, heat buildup in brake components throttles performance over extended descents, and frame geometry together with suspension dynamics shifts the rider’s weight distribution, compromising optimal brake application; consequently, while empirical data and modelling suggest that peak deceleration is attainable under controlled laboratory settings, real‑world constraints—including rider technique, terrain irregularities, and equipment wear—systematically quell the practical realization of such limits, underscoring the need for a holistic design and operational strategy that recognises and mitigates these multifaceted impediments.
