Optical rotation arises when plane-polarised light passes through a solution containing chiral molecules, and its magnitude is described by Biot's law, α = [α]lc, where α is the observed rotation, [α] the specific rotation, l the path length, and c the concentration. Glucose is dextrorotatory ([α] ≈ +52.7°) whereas fructose is strongly laevorotatory ([α] ≈ −92.4°), so a mixed solution produces a net rotation equal to the sum of the individual contributions. This additivity allows polarimetry to determine the composition of mixtures such as invert sugar, formed by the acid-catalysed hydrolysis of sucrose. In this investigation, rotations of glucose–fructose solutions of known concentration were measured using a polarimeter at a fixed wavelength (typically the sodium D-line, 589 nm) and controlled temperature, since both variables affect specific rotation.
