While artificial neural networks are loosely inspired by the structure of biological neurons, their architecture diverges from the brain in several important ways, offering a compelling subject for analysis. Biological systems consist of approximately 86 billion neurons, each forming thousands of synaptic connections through complex dendritic trees and chemical signalling mechanisms that operate in parallel and asynchronously, enabling remarkable energy efficiency and generalisation from sparse data. In contrast, artificial neural networks employ simplified mathematical models organised into discrete layers, typically using backpropagation and gradient descent to adjust weights during training. Although modern architectures such as convolutional and transformer networks have achieved impressive performance in specialised domains, they remain narrow in scope, lack the brain’s continual learning ability, and require vastly greater computational resources, highlighting fundamental differences rather than true equivalence.
