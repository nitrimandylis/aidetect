Cellular automata, particularly those modeled after von Neumann's early conceptualizations and exemplified by Wolfram's Rule 30, offer a compelling computational framework for simulating biological replication and evolution. Analysis reveals that these systems generate self-replicating patterns—such as the "breeder" configurations—that inherently demonstrate replication through rule-based interaction, while emergent properties of rule complexity can foster genetic diversity and adaptive structures under dynamic rule manipulation, effectively mirroring evolutionary pressures. This emergent, rule-driven succession inherently demonstrates how simple, deterministic processes can yield complex, adaptive phenomena resembling replication and evolution, providing a compelling computational analogue for studying these fundamental biological dynamics without direct biological components.
