The theory predicts that for a cylinder to roll without slipping, the static friction force must be sufficient to provide the necessary torque, and the linear acceleration is given by \(a = \frac{2}{3}g\sin\theta\). However, when slipping occurs, the friction coefficient is reduced to its kinetic value, \(\mu_k\), which is typically lower than the static coefficient. Consequently, the linear acceleration should increase because the frictional torque, which opposes rotational acceleration, is diminished. This leads to a greater discrepancy between the translational and rotational velocities; the cylinder’s centre of mass translates faster while its angular velocity lags behind the value required for pure rolling. This partial decoupling allows for a more direct conversion of gravitational potential energy into translational kinetic energy, fundamentally altering the object’s dynamic behaviour down the incline.
