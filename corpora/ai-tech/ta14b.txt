During repeated braking trials, the maximum deceleration achieved before rear-wheel lift was consistently lower than the theoretical limit predicted by the bicycle’s geometry and coefficient of friction, indicating a significant influence of rider position and suspension dynamics. Primarily, the front suspension fork compressed under load transfer, shifting the centre of mass forward and reducing the normal force on the rear wheel, which triggered lift at decelerations below the calculated static threshold. Furthermore, variations in body position—particularly arm extension and torso angle—altered the effective height of the centre of mass, directly affecting the pitch-over moment. The data also revealed that progressive brake modulation, intended to avoid abrupt fork dive, paradoxically lengthened stopping distance because the tyre could not maintain peak friction for the full duration, suggesting that maximum braking is constrained less by tyre grip than by the dynamic stability limits of the rider–bicycle system.
