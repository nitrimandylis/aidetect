The giant freshwater prawn Macrobrachium rosenbergii exhibits pronounced territorial behaviour that is closely bound up with competition for space, shelter and access to mates. Individuals, and particularly the larger dominant males, tend to establish and defend discrete areas, responding to the approach of conspecifics with a graded sequence of agonistic displays that ranges from the raising and extension of the chelae to direct physical contact. Such encounters typically culminate in the retreat of the subordinate animal, so that overt fighting is comparatively brief and the established hierarchy is subsequently maintained through ritualised signalling rather than continual combat. This territoriality has clear adaptive value, since the successful defence of a refuge reduces the risk of predation and cannibalism during the vulnerable period following moulting. Understanding these behavioural patterns is therefore of considerable importance, both for the interpretation of the species' natural ecology and for its increasingly widespread cultivation in aquaculture, where stocking density strongly influences aggression.
