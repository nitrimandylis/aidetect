The Dreyfus Affair cannot be understood in isolation from the broader tensions that characterised France in the 1890s, for the conviction of Alfred Dreyfus in 1894 acted less as a cause than as a catalyst that exposed divisions already present within the Third Republic. The lingering humiliation of defeat in the Franco-Prussian War had heightened both militarism and nationalism, while widespread antisemitism, given intellectual respectability by figures such as Édouard Drumont, provided a ready framework in which a Jewish officer could be presumed guilty. At the same time, the Republic remained bitterly contested between a Catholic, monarchist and army-aligned right and an anticlerical, republican left. The Affair therefore became the arena in which these opposing camps confronted one another, and its progress reveals how questions of individual justice were rapidly subsumed into a wider struggle over the identity and legitimacy of the French state itself.
