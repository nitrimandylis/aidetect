While it is tempting to attribute the low retention of indigenous students in Queensland schools primarily to the remoteness of the communities in which many of them live, the evidence suggests that geographical isolation offers only a partial explanation. It is true that distance from well-resourced secondary schools, the limited availability of specialist teachers and the cost of boarding away from home all present real obstacles to sustained attendance. However, retention rates remain comparatively low even among indigenous students in urban and regional centres where such physical barriers are largely absent, which indicates that other factors are at work. These include socio-economic disadvantage, the frequent mismatch between the curriculum and indigenous cultural experience, the effects of intergenerational disengagement from formal education, and, in some cases, a school environment perceived as unwelcoming. Remoteness therefore compounds a set of underlying social and cultural difficulties rather than functioning as their fundamental cause.
