Marlowe's Doctor Faustus draws heavily on the structure and moral machinery of the medieval morality play, yet it complicates the inherited form in ways that reflect a shifting attitude towards spirituality at the close of the sixteenth century. The Good and Evil Angels, the procession of the Seven Deadly Sins, and the final descent into damnation are all conventions the earlier tradition would have used to affirm a stable providential order and the certainty of redemption for the repentant. In Marlowe's hands, however, these devices frame a protagonist who remains suspended between belief and despair, repeatedly persuaded that his sin is too great to be forgiven. The result is a drama that retains the outward apparatus of orthodox instruction while dramatising a genuine anxiety about grace, free will and the reach of divine mercy, an anxiety characteristic of the Reformation culture in which the play was written.
