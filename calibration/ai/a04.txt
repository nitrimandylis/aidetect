The early institutionalisation of football in Costa Rica illustrates how a popular sport could be harnessed by the state as an instrument of social control and national integration. As the game spread beyond its origins among the urban elite in the first decades of the twentieth century, governing authorities recognised its capacity to organise leisure time, instil discipline and channel the energies of a growing working class into supervised and respectable activity. Municipal support for clubs and pitches, together with the framing of matches as occasions of civic pride, allowed football to be presented as a school of order and citizenship. At the same time, the emergence of a national team and of regional rivalries offered a shared vocabulary through which a still-consolidating sense of Costa Rican identity could be expressed. In this way sport operated simultaneously as a means of regulating the population and of cultivating loyalty to the nation.
